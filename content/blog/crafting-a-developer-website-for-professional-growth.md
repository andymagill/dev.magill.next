A professional website has become one of the most effective ways for developers and engineers to showcase their skills and experience. When [building my website](https://magill.dev/post/lets-breakdown-my-website), I got curious about what actually makes a developer site worth visiting. There's no shortage of beautiful portfolios and blogs out there, but what actually catches an employer's or client's eye?

In a competitive job market, a strong portfolio can set you apart. It introduces you to potential employers and clients before you ever meet them. Unlike a resume's list of bullet points, a portfolio lets people see how you actually work. A polished, engaging site can lead to real opportunities: job offers, consulting work, or freelance gigs. It's your chance to control the story of who you are as a developer and why someone should want to work with you.

### Show Growth, Not Projects

A good developer site does more than show off your best work — it's also about sharing your thought process and how you tackle challenges. **[Addy Osmani](https://addyosmani.com/blog/write-learn/)**, an Engineering Manager at Google, talks about how highlighting what you learned can set you apart: _"The practice of writing about learning encourages a growth mindset. It fosters curiosity, critical thinking, and a willingness to engage with challenging subjects."_ He suggests including details about the obstacles you faced and how you solved them. That way, you're not just showcasing your code — you're showing how you think.

### Storytelling and Your Personal Brand

A blog is your chance to tell your story — think of it as your origin story, minus the radioactive spider bite. It's where your projects, creativity, and personality meet. As **[Brad Frost](https://bradfrost.com/blog/post/write-on-your-own-website/)**, creator of [Atomic Design](https://atomicdesign.bradfrost.com/), writes: _"Writing on your own website associates your thoughts and ideas with you as a person."_ A good blog shows how you think, how you solve problems, and why you're the kind of developer people want to collaborate with.

### The Self-Closing Tag

Your site should reflect your journey as a developer, not a static resume you update once a year. Keep writing, keep adding what you're currently working on, and let it change as you do. That kind of upkeep is what turns a portfolio into a real showcase of not just what you do, but how well you do it.

These ideas, along with the resources below, helped shape how I built [my website](https://magill.dev/) and [work portfolio](https://magill.dev/projects).
